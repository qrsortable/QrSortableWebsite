## The hidden cost of unlabeled boxes

Whether you run a warehouse, pack event gear, stock a medical practice or ship e-commerce orders, you have probably lived this: you know the item exists, you just don’t know *which box* it’s in.

The delay is rarely the few seconds it takes to lift a lid. It’s the searching, the double-checking, the shuffling and the mistakes that follow. Research on warehouse order picking backs this up: one academic breakdown puts **travel at about 55%** and **searching at about 15%** of picking time, and picking itself can eat up to [60% of warehouse labor](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.00167).

Retail shows the same pattern. The Auburn University RFID Lab has long put average retail inventory accuracy at [around 65%](https://www.zebra.com/content/dam/zebra_dam/en/white-paper/retail-white-paper-inventory-visibility-retail-touchpoints-en-us.pdf). When records and reality drift apart, people compensate by checking, and checking costs time.

So we asked professionals a simple question: **how much time could you save if you knew exactly what was inside every box before opening it?**

## The common theme across every industry

The experts work in completely different worlds, yet they named the same benefits:

- Faster retrieval of items
- Less searching and guesswork
- Fewer mistakes and returns
- Better inventory accuracy
- Lower operational costs
- Higher productivity
- Less stress and decision fatigue

Runbo Li’s “cognitive inventory cost” explains why this matters beyond the clock. Every unidentified box is a small open question, and hundreds of open questions add up to friction. **The faster you know what’s inside a box, the faster you can decide and get work done.**

## Frequently asked questions

How much time can box labeling save?

Experts here reported a 25% cut in average pick time, a 77% drop in weekly receiving time, and at least 50% faster item finding. Results depend on how disorganized things were to begin with.

Why does searching cost so much?

In order picking, searching alone has been estimated at around 15% of the time, on top of travel. Removing guesswork shortens both.

What should a box label include?

A short code, the contents, and where the box belongs. Callum Gracie labels cases with contents and venue destination; Jake Wardle uses a clear code plus contents on every bin and box.

Why QR codes instead of a handwritten list?

A QR code points to details you can update at any time, including photos, notes and location, so the label never goes stale when the contents change.

How accurate is the average retailer’s inventory?

The Auburn University RFID Lab has reported around 65%, which is why many teams re-verify instead of trusting the record.
